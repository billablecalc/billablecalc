Hourly vs Fixed Price vs Retainer: Which Pricing Model Is Best?

There is no single best way to charge clients. Hourly, fixed price and retainer models each shift risk and income stability in different ways. Understanding the trade-offs lets you pick the right model for each client, and often combine all three.

Hourly pricing

You charge for the time you spend, usually with a time log and a monthly or weekly invoice.

Best for: unclear scope, ongoing support, discovery work and projects that may change.

Advantages: simple to start, fair when requirements change, and the client carries the risk of delays.

Disadvantages: the faster you work, the less you earn, and some clients worry about the total cost. You also have to track time carefully.

Fixed price

You agree one price for a defined result, regardless of how long it takes.

Best for: clearly defined projects with a known outcome.

Advantages: clients like the certainty, and you can earn more by working efficiently.

Disadvantages: scope creep. If a project needs more time than you estimated, you carry the cost.

A simple example: a project quoted at 2,000 for about 40 hours at 50 per hour. If you finish in 30 hours, you earn about 66.67 per hour. If it takes 60 hours, you earn about 33.33. Hourly billing would pay 1,500 and 3,000 for the same two cases.

Retainer

The client pays a fixed monthly fee for a set amount of time or a set service, such as 20 hours a month or a weekly content package.

Best for: ongoing work with clients you want to keep.

Advantages: predictable income, easier planning, and a deeper relationship with the client.

Disadvantages: unused hours may cause disputes, and you must be available. If the client needs more, you need a clear rule for extra work.

Example: 20 hours at 60 with a 10% retainer discount is 1,080 per month, or 12,960 a year. The client gets a lower rate in exchange for the commitment, and you get stable income.

How to choose

Ask three questions about each project:

How clear is the scope? If it is unclear, use hourly. If it is clear, use fixed price.

Is the relationship ongoing? If yes, consider a retainer.

How much risk can you carry? If you are new to a type of work, hourly or a larger buffer is safer.

Mixing models

Many freelancers combine them. A common pattern is a fixed-price discovery phase, a fixed-price build and then a monthly retainer for maintenance. Another is a retainer that covers a set number of hours, with extra work billed hourly.

Whatever you choose, write it down

Your contract should state the model, the rate or price, what is included, how extra work is handled and when invoices are due. Clear terms prevent most arguments.
