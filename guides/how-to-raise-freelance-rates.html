How to Raise Your Freelance Rates Without Losing Clients

Many freelancers keep the same rate for years because they fear losing clients. But costs go up, your skills grow and a rate that was fair when you started may now be too low. Raising rates is a normal business decision, and it can be done calmly.

Signs it is time

You are fully booked or have a waiting list.

Clients accept your quotes without any pushback.

Your skills or results have clearly improved since you set the rate.

A year has passed since your last increase.

Your costs have risen.

The math of a raise

A rate increase pays for itself faster than most people expect. Suppose you bill 1,200 hours a year at 50. Raising the rate to 55 adds 6,000 a year at the same workload. Even if the increase made you lose a client, you would need to lose about 9 percent of your billable work before you earned less. In many cases you can lose a few low-paying clients and still come out ahead, with time free for better work.

How much to raise

A common range is 5 to 15 percent for existing clients and a larger step, even 20 percent or more, for new clients. If you are very busy, a bigger step is justified. Round numbers are easier for clients to accept than odd ones.

How to tell existing clients

Give notice, typically 30 to 60 days, so the client can plan. Keep the message short and positive. For example: "Starting [DATE], my rate will be [NEW RATE] per hour. Projects already agreed stay at the current price. Thank you for your continued trust, I enjoy working with you." Do not apologize, do not over-explain and do not offer a long list of reasons.

Protect existing projects

Keep the old price for projects that are already contracted. Apply the new rate to new work. This avoids arguments and shows fairness.

For new clients

Quote the new rate from today. New clients have no reference point, so you can change your price whenever you want. Test it for a few proposals and watch the response.

If a client pushes back

Stay friendly. You can offer a smaller increase, a transition period or a package that reduces the number of hours rather than the rate. If a client cannot afford the new price, you can also decide to part ways kindly. Not every client is the right one forever.

Beyond hourly rates
